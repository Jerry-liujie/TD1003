\section{Introduction}\label{gen:sec:intro}

Chapter~3 ended at a ceiling. Synthesis-based search proved that
whole-query rewriting needs no rules, but its guidance is structural,
and structure runs out: as queries grow, candidate combinations
explode and the dataflow signal flattens, so SlabCity~\cite{dong2023slabcity} optimizes
just 2 of the 22 TPC-H~\cite{tpch} queries and 3 of the 99 far more
complex TPC-DS~\cite{tpcds} queries. Rules fail for lack of foresight;
search fails for lack of strength. Yet the queries both approaches
miss do get rewritten, every day, by human experts, which means the
knowledge exists. It is just not written down as rules, and not
reachable by enumeration. The question this chapter takes up is
whether that knowledge can be borrowed: large language models (LLMs)
are trained on the accumulated record of how people write and repair
SQL, and researchers have already applied them to
Text-to-SQL~\cite{fu2023catsql,gao2023text,sun2306sql,katsogiannis2023survey,pourreza2024din}
and other database
tasks~\cite{narayan2022can,suhara2022annotating,li2023table,lu2024large,zhou2023d}.
If their broad knowledge and reasoning can be made dependable for
rewriting, the queries beyond both rules and search come within reach.
To the best of our knowledge, the work in this chapter is the first to
focus on answering this question.

Dependable is the operative word, and manual rewriting shows what the
bar is. The expensive queries that matter most tend to be the complex
ones; queries auto-generated by BI tools such as
Looker~\cite{lookergoogle} routinely span hundreds of lines, and
verifying that a rewrite of such a query preserves its semantics
demands deep knowledge of the schema, the constraints, and the user
intent, which makes expert rewriting slow and error-prone even before
multiplying it by the thousands of slow queries a real organization
accumulates. An LLM that merely produces plausible-looking SQL does
not clear this bar. Concretely, five challenges stand between na\"ive
prompting and a usable system:
\begin{enumerate}[label=C\arabic*, leftmargin=0.5cm]

\item Simply asking the model to ``rewrite the given query into a
faster but equivalent form'' collapses on complex queries: most
outputs either fail to parse (syntactic errors) or silently change the
results (semantic errors), a product of SQL's intricacy and the
model's tendency to hallucinate (\cref{gen:sec:expr:llmbaseline}).

\item The model has no database cost model and cannot run experiments,
so even its correct rewrites are not reliably faster ones.

\item Invocation costs amortize over a recurrent workload, but
undisciplined invocation can still spend more on the model than the
resulting speedups save.

\item Hints improve the model's output, but too many hints, or
irrelevant ones, degrade it.

\item A model's rewrite arrives without a rationale, making it harder
for a human to understand and verify than a rule-produced one.

\end{enumerate}

\genhead{Our approach}
The system this chapter presents, \genrewrite, is the first holistic
answer to these challenges, and its central idea is that rules deserve
a second life in a new form. Whenever a rewrite succeeds, \genrewrite
has the model itself distill the insight behind it into a Natural
Language Rewrite Rule (NLR2): a textual strategy recording what
bottleneck was resolved and how. NLR2s repay their cost three ways.
Supplied as targeted hints, they help the model find better rewrites
in fewer invocations (C2, C4); being human-readable, they give every
rewrite an explanation a reviewer can check (C5); and, most
importantly, they transfer what was learned on one query to the next,
so the system grows more capable over the life of a workload (C1, C2,
C3). Because NLR2s carry no confidential information (such as column
or schema names), a repository trained on one workload can even
bootstrap \genrewrite on another. Two further mechanisms keep the loop
disciplined. Each NLR2 carries a utility score, so that only the rules
most relevant to the query at hand enter the prompt and redundant
rules do not confuse the model. And incorrect rewrites are not
discarded but repaired: a counterexample-guided correction procedure
iteratively removes their syntactic and semantic errors, minimizing
both model cost and the human effort of manual verification (C1, C3).

\genhead{High-level workflow}
At the workload level, \genrewrite takes as input a
workload,\footnote{A streaming setting is a special case, whereby
queries are presented and rewritten one at a time.} expressed as a set
of input queries $\mathcal{Q}$, and returns \{($q$,$q'$,$e$) \textbar
$q$ $\in$ $\mathcal{Q}_{opt}$\}, where
$\mathcal{Q}_{opt}\subseteq \mathcal{Q}$ is the optimizable subset of
the input queries, $q'$ is the rewritten query that is equivalent to
$q$ but likely to run faster, and $e$ is a human-readable explanation
of the rewrite. \Cref{gen:fig:arch} shows the iterative workflow. In
each iteration, every query in the workload passes through three
phases to find a better rewrite than the one found in previous
iterations: {\small \Circled{1}} rewrite suggestion, which selects
appropriate NLR2s from the repository to serve as hints and deposits
any newly discovered NLR2 back into it; {\small \Circled{2}} rewrite
correction, which iteratively refines rewrites based on
counterexamples provided by the LLM to eliminate semantic and
syntactic errors; and {\small \Circled{3}} rewrite evaluation, which
checks the rewrites for equivalence and performance and updates the
benefit scores of the NLR2s that were used. Iterations continue until
no additional improvement is found and the set of rewrites stabilizes.
The rewrites then go through a final verification step
{\small \Circled{4}}: where the formal verifier cannot guarantee
equivalence automatically, a human expert inspects and confirms the
rewritten query's equivalence, aided by its explanation, before it is
deployed to production.

\genhead{Target workloads}
The economics govern the scope. Model invocations and occasional human
inspection are one-time overheads, so \genrewrite targets exactly the
settings where they amortize:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=0.5cm]
\item \textbf{Recurrent workloads}, where the same query template is
executed many times, such as BI (Business Intelligence) dashboards,
reporting, ETL, DB-based applications, and dbt~\cite{dbt} jobs; over
60\% of the jobs in Microsoft clusters, for instance, are
recurrent~\cite{jindal2018selecting}. Here, queries are rewritten once
and the benefit is reaped on every subsequent execution.
\item \textbf{Highly expensive queries} expected to take tens of
minutes, such as an adhoc query scanning terabytes of data, where even
a single execution dwarfs the overhead of the LLM and the human
expert.
\end{enumerate}
In other words, \genrewrite is \emph{not} designed for real-time adhoc
workloads, where queries are novel \textbf{\underline{and}} relatively
light, as the additional overhead cannot be amortized.

\genhead{Contributions}
In summary, this chapter makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=0.5cm]
\item We present one of the first in-depth analyses of LLMs for query
rewriting, outlining key challenges and opportunities for future
research in this area (\cref{gen:sec:insight}).
\item We present \genrewrite, a holistic tool leveraging LLMs for
autonomous query rewriting. It addresses the key challenges of using
LLMs by introducing (i) the notion of Natural Language Rewrite Rules
(NLR2) for finding better rewrites and effective knowledge transfer,
(ii) a utility score for NLR2s to minimize LLM confusion caused by
irrelevant or too many rules, and (iii) a counterexample-guided
iterative correction method (\cref{gen:sec:approach}).
\item We evaluate \genrewrite on the standard TPC-DS and JOB
benchmarks and their SQLStorm-generated variants, where it
consistently optimizes more queries than every baseline at every
speedup threshold; at the $\geq$2x threshold on TPC-DS it improves 25
queries, about 1.35x more than LLM-driven baselines and 2.6x more than
LLM-enhanced rule-based ones, with the gap widening further on TPC-DS
(SQLStorm) (\cref{gen:sec:expr}).
\end{enumerate}

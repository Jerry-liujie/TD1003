Our experiments are designed to answer the following questions:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]  \setlength\itemsep{2pt}
\item 
\textbf{Coverage:} 
How does $\toolname$'s \emph{coverage} compare against that of state-of-the-art query rewriting techniques? 
That is, which technique can optimize more queries? 
(Section~\ref{slab:sec:q1-coverage}) 
\item 
\textbf{Query latency reduction:}
How does $\toolname$ compare to state-of-the-art query rewriters in terms of the efficiency of the generated queries? 
That is, for input queries that can be rewritten, which technique leads to faster queries? 
(Section~\ref{slab:sec:q2-reduction})
 
\item
\textbf{Ablation study:} How important are various ideas in \slabcity? 
(Section~\ref{slab:sec:ablation})

\end{itemize}



\subsection{Experimental Setup}
%In what follows, we describe our workloads, data generation method, baselines, and experiment environment.

\head{Testbed}
All experiments, for \toolname and baselines alike, including all
query executions, run on Amazon EC2 r5.large instances (16GB RAM,
200GB gp2 SSD, Xeon E5-2686 v4 CPU, Ubuntu 22.04) against PostgreSQL
12.13.

\head{Workloads}
We use four workloads of different character:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item 
\textbf{LeetCode.}
Real queries by real developers: we crawled every publicly available
SQL solution accepted as correct by LeetCode~\cite{leetcode},
manually filtered out solutions that are in fact wrong despite being
accepted (LeetCode's own tests are incomplete), and formalized the
schemas and integrity constraints of every task, yielding a curated
suite of 1131 queries. Many accepted solutions are visibly
novice-written and slow, which is precisely what makes the suite
valuable for evaluating rewriters.

\begin{comment}
\cancut{Overall, the queries were quite diverse and non-trivial. On our 10M database, \todo{xx} queries do not terminate within 10 hours, and \todo{xx} queries take at least \todo{xx} seconds to finish. Even on our smallest database with 100K rows, \todo{xx} queries take more than \todo{xx} seconds.}
\end{comment}



\item 
\textbf{Calcite.}
All 794 queries from Calcite's optimization-rules test
suite~\cite{calcite-test-suite}, previously used for rewriter
evaluation in~\cite{wetune}.

\item 
\textbf{TPC.}
The standard TPC-H~\cite{tpc-h} and TPC-DS~\cite{tpc-ds} analytical
benchmarks.

\end{itemize}


\head{Data generation}
For \leetcode\ we generate databases of 100K, 1M, and 10M rows; for
\calcite, 250K, 1M, and 4M, the last being the largest that fits in
16GB of memory. Following~\cite{wetune}, each database comes in a
uniform and a Zipfian variant (skew parameter 1.25, as in that work),
and all generated data respects the integrity constraints. TPC-H and
TPC-DS data comes from the official generators at scale factor 1 (1GB
of data), matching the setup of $\learnedrewrite$~\cite{LR}.

\head{Baselines}
We compare against two state-of-the-art rule-based query rewriters:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item
\textbf{\learnedrewrite} (or \LR, VLDB 2022~\cite{LR,LR-source-code})
steers the application of \calcite's rule set using Monte Carlo tree
search, and was shown to outperform multiple earlier
systems~\cite{begoli2018apache,pirahesh1992extensible}.

\item 
\textbf{\wetune} (or \WT, SIGMOD 2022~\cite{wetune,WT-source-code})
is the state-of-the-art automatic discoverer of rewrite rules; the
dozens of new rules it found yield optimizations missed by existing
rule sets and systems such as MS SQL Server.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Coverage}
\label{slab:sec:q1-coverage}

The first question is how many queries from each workload each
technique can optimize.

\head{Setup}
For every input query $\inputquery$ (with its schema) we run each
technique, \toolname with its 5-second budget, and obtain an output
query $\query$. We then execute input and output queries three times
each, restarting the PostgreSQL service and clearing the database
cache before every run, average the latencies, and count $\inputquery$
as optimized if $\query$ is faster. A 10-hour execution timeout
applies; outputs exceeding it count as not optimized. Baselines use
the identical setup.

\evalfinding{\emph{\toolname can optimize more queries across different workloads.}}

\head{Results}
Figure~\ref{slab:fig:leetcode-results}(a) and
Figure~\ref{slab:fig:calcite-results}(a) show coverage for all
techniques on the uniform distribution. On LeetCode at 10M rows, for
instance, \toolname optimizes 222 input queries against
$\learnedrewrite$'s 132 and $\wetune$'s 20. Results on the Zipfian
distribution are similar (Table~\ref{slab:tab:zipfian-results}).
Because only a subset of \toolname's outputs is fully verified, we
manually inspected every output query that passed only the tester and
bounded verifier, and confirmed that all optimizations counted here
are genuine equivalences; \cref{slab:sec:eval:discussion} discusses
the limits of full verification at length. On TPC-H, \toolname
optimizes 2 of the 22 queries (\#17 and \#20) within 5 seconds, the
same two as $\LR$, while $\WT$ optimizes none. On the far more
complex TPC-DS, \toolname optimizes three (\#1, \#30, \#81); $\LR$
optimizes two of them (\#1, \#30) with markedly slower outputs, and
$\WT$ again none.

\head{Discussion}
The margin over the baselines is much wider on LeetCode than on
Calcite, which is the expected signature: Calcite's queries are the
home turf of Calcite-derived rule sets, while LeetCode's larger
queries, heavy with aggregation and window functions, present exactly
the unseen patterns rule sets miss. The gap is evidence for both the
dataset's usefulness and the approach's premise. The baselines do
cover a few queries \toolname misses: some use operators our
prototype does not yet support (coalesce, for example), and some fall
to the 5-second budget. Supporting more operators and parallelizing
the search are engineering paths that address both.

\subsection{Query Latency Reduction}
\label{slab:sec:q2-reduction}

Coverage says \toolname optimizes more queries; this subsection asks
whether it also optimizes them better, comparing the performance of
its outputs against the baselines' outputs.

\head{Setup}
Using the setup of \cref{slab:sec:q1-coverage}, we measure each
technique's latency reduction ratio: for an input $\inputquery$ and
output $\query$, a reduction of 5x means $\query$ is five times
faster. A timed-out query enters the ratio with the 10-hour timeout as
its latency; pairs where both input and output time out are excluded.

\evalfinding{\emph{\toolname generates faster queries across different workloads.}}




\head{Results}
Figure~\ref{slab:fig:leetcode-results}(b) and
Figure~\ref{slab:fig:calcite-results}(b) report the reduction ratios,
with geometric means as well as medians and 75th percentiles. On
LeetCode at 10M, \toolname's geometric-mean reduction across all its output
queries is 50.3x, against 1.4x for $\LR$ and 12x for $\WT$, and its
lead holds across both workloads and all data sizes. Among the queries
\toolname covers, the median reduction reaches 1.9x on LeetCode and
2.2x on Calcite. The Zipfian distribution again agrees
(Table~\ref{slab:tab:zipfian-results}). On TPC-H, \toolname and
$\LR$ both achieve speedups of more than an order of magnitude. On
TPC-DS, \toolname optimizes one query $\LR$ cannot, produces faster
outputs on the two queries both can handle, and accelerates all three
of its optimized queries by at least an order of magnitude.

\head{Discussion}
As with coverage, the LeetCode margins exceed the Calcite margins, for
the same home-turf reason: the baselines' rule sets are designed
around Calcite-style queries, and LeetCode supplies what they have not
seen. One apparent anomaly deserves explanation: \toolname's mean
reduction on LeetCode 10M is lower than on 1M. The cause is 15 input
queries (all from problem \#1308) that exceed the 10-hour timeout at
both sizes and therefore enter the ratio with identical 10-hour
numerators, while \toolname's outputs for them run in 3 seconds at 1M
but over 30 seconds at 10M; the denominators grow while the numerators
cannot, and the 10M mean shrinks.

\head{Impact of CEGIS}
Does counterexample reuse earn its place? With it, \toolname examines
437 candidates on average within 5 seconds on LeetCode while
generating only 1.4 counterexamples; a variant that discards each
counterexample after one use manages only 175 candidates in the same
time. The saved time is exactly the tester time not spent regenerating
refutations that old counterexamples replay for free, confirming in
this domain the efficiency observation that motivated
CEGIS~\cite{solar2008program}.

\head{Impact of query testing}
The frequency of verifier calls is the cost that must be controlled:
within one second the tester can refute several dozen candidates,
while the verifier handles at most a couple. Without the testing
layer, verification alone would throttle the search.

\head{Impact of using dataflows to guide search}
A variant of \slabcity with the dataflow guidance removed, enumerating
candidates by size as standard synthesis does, optimizes none of our
input queries within the 5-second budget. Of all the ablations this is
the starkest: without the abstraction of \cref{slab:sec:dataflow},
nothing else in the system has time to matter.

\head{Impact of using cost estimates for ranking}
\texttt{EXPLAIN} is known to give inaccurate cost estimates, so we
compare production \slabcity against a variant that ranks by true
measured latency, obtained by running every candidate against the
database: an impractical oracle, but an informative ceiling.
Table~\ref{slab:tab:exact-latencies} compares the two in coverage and
in latency reduction. Ranking by exact latency increases coverage,
substantially so on some LeetCode settings, while the geometric-mean
latency reductions of the two methods remain broadly comparable and
neither ranking dominates every setting. Thus \texttt{EXPLAIN}'s
estimates trade some coverage for much cheaper candidate ranking.

\head{Percentage of fully verified rewrites}
A subset of \slabcity's rewrites clears the full verifier, carrying
the strongest guarantee possible, equivalence over all inputs: on
LeetCode 100K, that subset is 17\% (verified by \cosette and \spes).
What the remaining rewrites' labels mean is the subject of the next
subsection.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{slab:sec:eval:discussion}


The 17\% full-verification rate invites a misreading that is worth
correcting with the frame Chapter~2 established: guarantees come in
degrees, and the relevant comparison is not against perfection but
against the alternative. Rule-based rewriting inherits its correctness
from its rules, and the vast majority of rules in deployed rule sets
are themselves not formally
verified~\cite{chu2017hottsql,chu2018axiomatic}; they rest on
error-prone human judgment. The consequences are measurable: about
5\% of the rewrites $\learnedrewrite$ produced from the Calcite rule
set were proved \emph{incorrect} by \toolname's own checker, whereas
every \toolname output passes that same checker. The few prior
approaches that do offer formal guarantees have done so at very small
scale, such as the 7 queries of the FGH
work~\cite{wang2022optimizing}. And for \slabcity's target
deployments, described in \cref{slab:sec:intro}, queries that are
rerun many times and inspected once before entering service, such as
BI dashboards, a bounded-verification label is a workable outcome: the
human inspection happens exactly where a human was already in the
loop, at query-definition time and before production.

The tester and bounded verifier also do quiet work on the other side
of the ledger. First, they eliminate incorrect rewrites very
effectively, by more than 80\%, so that human experts inspect a short
list of high-quality rewrites rather than a stream of near-misses; the
5\% of $\LR$'s rewrites our checker disproves illustrates the same
filter applied outward. Second, surviving the two layers is itself
strong evidence: among \toolname's rewrites that pass, 97\% are
confirmed correct (by full verification or manual inspection) and only
3\% are found to be incorrect during manual adjudication. Those
checker-surviving but incorrect rewrites are excluded from the
optimization counts reported above.

Seen whole, the trade \toolname offers is optimization capability
against manual assurance overhead: it reaches rewrites that rule-based
systems structurally cannot, and pays for that reach with more
rewrites needing human sign-off, a cost that better automated
verifiers will progressively reduce. The approach is also
complementary rather than competitive: one can rely on rule-based
rewriting where its rules apply and invoke \toolname everywhere else,
taking the best of both.
